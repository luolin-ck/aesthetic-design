# Bauhaus: Design Philosophy and Style Guide

- **Name**: Bauhaus
- **Concept type**: A design school and the influence of its ideas, teaching, and making practices
- **Core definition**: Bring artistic creation, craft training, material experiments, and industrial production into one process, so that a product or layout explains its use and construction through form.

## Attribution Criteria

### 1. Scope and Claims
Separate three claims before calling a work Bauhaus:
- **Historical Bauhaus work**: A product of the school's lifetime (1919–1933) or a specific school workshop. Historical records, rather than appearance, must establish this identity.
- **Bauhaus-like visual language**: An observer may compare a contemporary result with known historical works without first obtaining the maker's reference list.
- **Creative sources or workshop methods**: Claims that the maker actually drew on a specific Bauhaus work or used a school workshop method require sketches or process records.

Examine component relationships in objects and furniture, layout in graphics, and functional divisions in spaces separately. Websites can be assessed only as contemporary translations.

### 2. Core Evidence Dimensions

| Core evidence | How to observe or verify | Why it distinguishes | Limits and alternative explanations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Historical connection between the original design and school practice | Check title, design date, the maker's school role, workshop or commission records, and museum research. Record original design and current manufacture dates separately. | Answers whether this is a historical Bauhaus work, beyond a generally modern appearance. | A designer's school affiliation does not make their entire career school work. Later editions need separate identification. |
| Form becomes a usable or manufacturable component | In objects, map container, handle, support, and joint roles; in graphics, map the information roles of headings, images, and color fields; in spaces, check functional plans and component relations. | Tests connections among form, material, and use rather than geometry applied to an ordinary surface. | Function, material experiments, and sans-serif type are shared with broader modernism and cannot identify Bauhaus alone. A photograph cannot prove manufacturing methods. |
| Structural correspondence with known historical examples | Place the contemporary result beside a specific museum example and identify component assemblies or color, line, and plane relationships translated to the new medium; woven warp/weft relations might inform information divisions. | Provides a checkable reference for a Bauhaus-like claim instead of vague rationality. | Resemblance is not causal influence; a maker's assertion is only a lead. If other modernist examples explain it equally well, use the broader category Modernist orientation. |

- **Supporting appearances**: Primary red/yellow/blue, circles/squares/triangles, thick black divisions, bent steel tubing, and geometric sans-serif type. Alone or together these do not establish attribution. Conversely, silver-and-black metalwork, textured textiles, and early Expressionist craft are not excluded for lacking primary colors.

### 3. Combined Judgment and Neighboring Styles
1. Distinguish the original design, the object in front of you, and a contemporary proposal.
   - For historical attribution, prioritize the first evidence row. Once records establish workshop practice, identify the teaching or workshop branch; absence of stereotypical colors does not overturn it.
   - For contemporary forms, compare with checkable historical examples. Component or layout relationships governing the main work support **Primary style: Bauhaus-like visual language**. Colors or generic geometric icons alone show only color/generic-motif resemblance. **Local borrowing** requires an identifiable organizing relationship or correspondence with a specific historical example in a limited area. Good function and material presence without specific historical correspondence support broader Modernism.
2. Compare [De Stijl](02-de-stijl.md) and [Modernism](04-modernism.md): can orthogonal abstract balance or general functional design explain the same evidence? Do not narrow to Bauhaus without a more specific correspondence.
3. Records locating a work at another institution can refute school-work identity without cancelling visual similarity. Missing records mean historical attribution is unverified, not disproved.

### 4. Worked Attribution Examples

| Case | Input facts → conclusion | Evidence and unresolved questions |
|---|---|---|
| Positive: Brandt teapot | The Met records a design around 1924 from the Bauhaus metal workshop. A hemispherical body, short spout, and ebony handle divide containment, pouring, and insulated grip → the original design's historical Bauhaus attribution is established, despite no red/yellow/blue. | Workshop records and geometric component roles agree. Failure to reach mass production does not cancel school identity. For a particular object, check production numbers and provenance to distinguish an early object from a later authorized reproduction. |
| Negative, invented | A 2026 shopping promotion uses ordinary centered text with randomly placed primary-colored basic shapes at its edges. Moving the shapes changes no information structure; historical comparisons show only generic geometry → **Attribution not supported** for the overall design; generic geometric decoration. | Centered typography governs the work; the shapes do not organize information. Specific museum correspondence might later support Local borrowing in a limited area. |
| Boundary, invented | A shop manufactures a steel-tube chair in 2026 from Marcel Breuer's historical drawings, with a label reading only “Bauhaus 1925” → the original design has historical connections, but this object is modern production/a reproduction, not a 1925 original. | Structural correspondence does not prove object age or authorization. Check manufacturer authorization and production records; modern manufacture changes the object's historical identity, not the original design's context. |

## Position and Background

Founded in Weimar in 1919, the Bauhaus changed across its Weimar, Dessau, and Berlin phases. Early emphasis on a craft-guild community moved toward prototypes for industrial production. Red/yellow/blue geometry overlooks its work in textiles, metalware, steel-tube furniture, photography, stage, and architecture. See [The Met: The Bauhaus, 1919–1933](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-bauhaus-1919-1933).

## Design Philosophy

1. **Begin with use and material properties**: Study load, grip, daylight, or reading before deriving geometry. Structure and touch belong to form, rather than a decorative covering.
2. **Verify form in workshops**: Connect the Vorkurs foundation course with specialist workshops; test color, proportion, and construction in prototypes, beyond drawings. See [Getty: Principles and Curriculum](https://www.getty.edu/research/exhibitions_events/exhibitions/bauhaus/new_artist/history/principles_curriculum/).
3. **Connect objects to a whole living system**: Vessels, furniture, print, and architecture can share organizing logic without having the same geometric silhouette.

## Design Language

> Contemporary applications below are practical suggestions, not a unified historical doctrine.

| Dimension | Typical language | Contemporary translation |
|---|---|---|
| Composition and layout | Basic geometry, asymmetric dynamic balance, clear structural hierarchy | Organize headings, images, and captions with shared grids and alignment. |
| Color | Primary-color experiments alongside metal, wood, and textile colors | Give saturated accents an information role rather than mechanically applying three primaries. |
| Typography | Sans-serif exploration, geometric simplification, information orientation | Keep body text readable; reserve geometric emphasis for appropriate headings. |
| Graphics and symbols | Circles, squares, lines, objective photo collage, direction signs | Use geometry to guide movement, divide modules, or explain structure. |
| Materials and texture | Steel tubing, glass, plywood, metal, and mixed-fiber weaving | Expose connections and material qualities; verify production conditions before design. |
| Space and interaction | Functional zoning, modular combinations, industrial assembly | Group digital tasks clearly, reuse components, and provide direct feedback. |

## Practical Design Methods

1. Define a testable functional problem, such as making course time and signup understandable to passersby in three seconds.
2. Set a small system of grid ratios, type levels, and repeatable modules shared by branding, packaging, and wayfinding.
3. Test real prototypes for grip, seating support, and circulation; test graphics and websites with real short and long text.
4. Keep digital controls usable: geometry never replaces explicit button labels, states, or adequate hit areas.

## Design Example

> **Contemporary translation, invented: community making-workshop course identity**
> - **Goal**: Let first-time residents see what crafts they can learn, when classes run, and how to register.
> - **Graphics and web**: Separate course name, time, and tool photographs into three levels. Circles identify craft categories; thick lines connect making steps. The website shares the grid and type hierarchy; registration uses direct text, not abstract geometric icons.
> - **Wayfinding**: Reusable timber-frame signs expose metal connectors. Shared information and assembly rules generate system consistency.

## Representative Cases and Application Boundaries

- **Cases**: Marcel Breuer's Wassily armchair and Marianne Brandt's metal tea service appear in [The Met's Bauhaus essay](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/the-bauhaus-1919-1933). The former separates the supporting roles of bent tubing and canvas straps; the latter makes spheres, cylinders, and discs into containers, openings, and bases.
- **Boundaries**: Useful for tools, craft education, exhibitions, and structurally rational identities. Historical narratives, local traditions, or personal drawing require cultural content beyond removing ornament. Industrial scalability is not the sole measure of craft value.

## Related Styles and Common Misreadings

- **Bauhaus and [De Stijl](02-de-stijl.md)**: An education–workshop–production system differs from abstract universal harmony in lines, planes, and colors, although their histories interacted.
- **Bauhaus and [Swiss Style](03-swiss-style.md)**: Bauhaus spans buildings, objects, textiles, stage, and print; Swiss Style concentrates on graphic grids, photography, and typography.
- **Misreadings**: Primary-colored shapes on a generic layout prove neither identity nor visual language. Function, honest materials, and clean layout establish only broad modern principles. School attribution needs records; visual language needs distinguishing correspondence with specific examples.
