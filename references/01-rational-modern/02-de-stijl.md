# De Stijl: Design Philosophy and Style Guide

- **Name**: De Stijl
- **Concept type**: A Dutch modern art and design movement
- **Core definition**: Build an abstract universal order through tensions and counterbalances among a few lines, color planes, and voids, extending two-dimensional composition into furniture and architecture.

## Attribution Criteria

### 1. Scope and Claims
- **Historical works**: De Stijl developed around its magazine, correspondence, and theoretical debate, including disagreements. It did not issue membership by a fixed visual formula. Verify dates, maker, exhibition/publication records, and scholarship.
- **Contemporary visual language**: Assess graphics, furniture, and spaces through observable formal relationships below.
- **Digital limits**: Websites and apps may borrow divisions and counterbalances of lines and planes. Rectangular buttons and grids do not establish De Stijl.

### 2. Core Evidence Dimensions

| Core evidence | How to observe or verify | Why it distinguishes | Limits and alternative explanations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lines, planes, and voids act as independent elements | In graphics, trace lengths, areas, positions, and edge tension. In furniture, inspect members extending beyond crossings and planes visually separate from frames. In spaces, trace extending and intersecting wall/color planes. | Nonfigurative relationships are the main subject, rather than simplified outlines of recognizable objects. | Abstract geometry is shared with other modernisms; squares or blocks alone are insufficient. |
| Unequal asymmetric counterbalance, rather than uniform filling | Map unequal color fields, voids, axes, and extensions; ask which opposition would change if one field moved or changed area. | Distinguishes dynamic nonmirror organization from equal decorative squares, tables, or data cells. | Whether the balance is beautiful is a quality judgment. An awkward imitation may still use recognizable counterbalancing rules. |
| Specific historical branch and its governing role | Compare Mondrian's orthogonal primary-color studies, Rietveld's separated three-dimensional components, or van Doesburg's diagonal counter-compositions; check references. | Prevents the best-known phase becoming the sole standard for the entire movement. | Primaries and right angles are strong leads, not mandatory in every historical work. Diagonals do not automatically refute a De Stijl connection. |

- **Supporting appearances**: Primary red/yellow/blue, black/white/gray, right angles, and heavy black divisions. Equal table cells filled with primaries, or an ordinary chair painted red and blue, show only similar colors.

### 3. Combined Judgment and Neighboring Styles
1. Separate historical attribution from visible form.
   - A documented member's work does not lose its historical identity because of diagonals or nonprimary colors; name its particular exploration. Without maker/date records, leave history unverified. A crop permits only a partial assessment.
   - Independent lines/planes, nonfigurative construction, and asymmetric dynamic counterbalance jointly governing the main work, checked against a historical branch, support **Primary style**. Primary colors or a generic small framework alone show only color/generic-motif resemblance. **Local borrowing** requires a recognizable line/plane counterbalancing relationship or specific historical correspondence in the limited area concerned.
2. Compare [Swiss Style](03-swiss-style.md): do divisions encode information hierarchy, or make line/plane relationships the subject?
3. Compare [Bauhaus](01-bauhaus.md): abstract balance does not establish school-workshop teaching or production identity.
4. Figurative narrative with small edge grids does not support De Stijl as the main language. Geometry and photographic narrative governing separate central areas can support **Mixed**. Never veto the whole movement for diagonals: [MoMA's research](https://www.moma.org/explore/inside_out/2010/03/03/moma-offsite-across-the-river-across-the-pond/) connects van Doesburg's diagonal work with De Stijl; Mondrian's personal rules cannot define all members.

### 4. Worked Attribution Examples

| Case | Input facts → conclusion | Evidence and unresolved questions |
|---|---|---|
| Positive: Rietveld's Red Blue Chair | MoMA records development during 1918–1923. Seat, back, and wooden frame remain independent; horizontal/vertical members extend beyond intersections and colored planes interlock with the black frame → historical attribution is established and form embodies the movement's spatial investigations. | Spatial intersections of independent members and planes are decisive; colors reinforce separation. A sales photograph cannot date a reproduction or establish authorization. |
| Negative, invented | Nine equal course cards in an educational website alternate red, yellow, and blue, with ordinary centered text → **Attribution not supported**; only primary-color resemblance. | Homogeneous data cells, rather than independent nonfigurative counterbalance, determine the grid. Usability is a separate question. |
| Boundary: van Doesburg's Simultaneous Counter-Composition | MoMA documents maker, 1929–1930 date, and diagonal dynamic exploration → discuss within De Stijl's historical practice and its Elementarist branch, not as compliance with Mondrian's pure orthogonality. | Identity and scholarship establish the connection; diagonals identify another theoretical branch. An anonymous diagonal crop alone establishes neither historical attribution nor De Stijl form. |

## Position and Background

Named after the magazine founded in the Netherlands in 1917, De Stijl includes Theo van Doesburg, Piet Mondrian, and Gerrit Rietveld. The [Städel Museum's guide](https://vasarely.staedelmuseum.de/en/) explains its pursuit of universal visual language beyond personal emotion, extended from painting to furniture, architecture, and living environments. Orthogonals and primaries are entry points, not an unchanging rule for every member and phase.

## Design Philosophy

1. **Relationships over depiction**: Organize lines, planes, color, and voids as interdependent abstraction rather than outlining natural objects.
2. **Dynamic balance through asymmetry**: Vary area, location, and color weight instead of mirroring both sides.
3. **Extend abstraction into living space**: Decompose furniture and architecture into independent planes and linear members. The [Red Blue Chair](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4044) turns two-dimensional relations into three-dimensional construction.

## Design Language

> Contemporary applications interpret formal logic; they are not reproduction standards.

| Dimension | Typical language | Contemporary translation |
|---|---|---|
| Composition and layout | Orthogonal divisions, unequal rectangles, asymmetric balance | Establish division ratios and open voids before placing content. |
| Color | Saturated primaries against black, white, and gray | Use a few fields as zones or anchors; retain strong text contrast. |
| Typography | Text blocks participate as rectangular elements | Group paragraphs along common axes and baselines; do not deform long text into geometry. |
| Graphics and symbols | Independent lines/planes with tense edge junctions | Extend lines across pages or modules, avoiding random decorative boxes. |
| Materials and texture | Clearly exposed, distinct roles for members, panels, and joints | Separate load frames and contact panels through material and color, rather than repainting generic furniture. |
| Space and interaction | Interpenetrating wall/partition planes balance flow and division | Use information layers while preserving predictable vertical scrolling and reading. |

## Practical Design Methods

1. Test in black and white first: adjust area, line weight, and voids until information emphasis survives color removal.
2. Limit line widths and module ratios across branding/media; do not frame every paragraph mechanically.
3. Bind colors to roles, such as red for main action/title, blue for category, yellow for status. Keep text labels for important states; do not require all primaries on every screen.
4. Coordinate walls, platforms, and signs as independent intersecting planes. Preserve continuous forms and long-text reading on digital screens instead of interrupting tasks with hard divisions.

## Design Example

> **Contemporary translation, invented: university architecture lectures and registration**
> - **Goal**: Combine spatial composition with clear signup guidance across a lecture series.
> - **Graphics and web**: A compact vertical title block, wide architectural photograph, and light lower-right time/location sidebar form unequal orthogonal balance. Red, blue, and yellow identify keynote, workshop series, and signup messages without appearing everywhere. Desktop registration preserves ratios; mobile becomes a clear vertical stack.
> - **Exhibition**: Open frames of thin black square tubing carry cantilevered colored information panels, echoing separation of lines and planes.

## Representative Cases and Application Boundaries

- **Case**: Rietveld's [Red Blue Chair](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4044) interlocks black wooden bars and painted panels. MoMA notes that structural design and the familiar colors developed at different times: style evolved rather than appearing as a finished formula.
- **Boundaries**: Useful for architecture exhibitions, art education, editorial books, and modular displays. Weaken hard divisions for very long text, dense data comparison, or warm natural settings; abstraction must not sacrifice readability and approachability.

## Related Styles and Common Misreadings

- **With [Bauhaus](01-bauhaus.md)**: Historical interaction does not equate universal abstract harmony with an integrated foundation-course/workshop/production education system.
- **With [Swiss Style](03-swiss-style.md)**: De Stijl makes asymmetric line/plane relationships the expressive subject; Swiss grids serve information hierarchy and typographic clarity.
- **Misreading**: A nine-cell grid in primaries is not enough. Check independent elements, unequal dynamic counterbalances, and a particular historical branch; generic balance and primary colors cannot establish attribution.
