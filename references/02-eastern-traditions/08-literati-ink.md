# Literati Ink: Design Philosophy and Style Guide

- **Search terms**: Chinese literati painting / Scholar painting
- **Concept type**: A Chinese cultural/visual tradition combining poetry, calligraphy, painting, and seals
- **Core definition**: Use calligraphic brush rhythms, concise inscriptions, and culturally meaningful natural imagery to express temperament, values, and historical echoes, rather than photographic likeness.

## Attribution Criteria

### 1. Scope and Claims
These working criteria concern paintings and contemporary graphic, brand, and digital borrowing; they are not a uniform test for every dynasty.
- **Historical attribution**: Consider biography, social networks, inscriptions, seals/provenance, and scholarship. Black ink does not prove a maker's scholar identity or character.
- **Contemporary expression**: Relationships among brushwork, imagery, and inscription can translate the tradition. An ink-landscape screen inside a room establishes only Local borrowing, not the whole architecture's style.
- **Handmaking and mental-state claims**: Actual brush-on-paper manufacture or personal reclusive feeling requires process records/interviews. Screen effects cannot prove an original or reveal someone's mind.

### 2. Core Evidence Dimensions

| Core evidence | How to observe or verify | Why it distinguishes | Limits and alternative explanations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calligraphic strokes organize image and rhythm | Enlarge starts/stops, movement, wet/dry and dark/light changes, texture strokes/dots forming rocks/plants, and transitions of density/void. | Brush movement has independently appreciable structure/rhythm, beyond coloring objects or mechanical outlines. | Digital tools simulate broken strokes and washes. An image cannot prove handmaking or calligraphic skill; one dry stroke cannot classify the whole. |
| Imagery, inscription, and traditional responses generate specific meaning | Read poems/inscriptions/copy with the image; examine bamboo/rocks, gardens, fishing, and responses to earlier models, identifying meanings text adds beyond depiction. | Distinguishes poetry/image interpretation from monochrome scenery or tourist diagrams. | Not every historical work has a long poem. Reliable scholarship can establish earlier brush traditions or cultural motifs without text; absence of a poem is not counterevidence. |
| Brushwork and interpretive relationships govern main expression | Inspect inscriptions, seals, main image, voids, and breathing rhythm; trace imagery through series or exhibition sequences. | Makes the borrowing a layout/narrative framework rather than an ink texture behind a template. | Text placed beside scenery may remain a generic guide. A historical scholar's identity does not replace analysis of this particular work. |

- **Supporting appearances**: Grayscale, brush lettering, red relief/intaglio seals, plum/orchid/bamboo/chrysanthemum, mountains/boats, paper fibers, and voids also occur in packaging, court painting, filters, and generic Chinese templates. Neither singly nor together do they certify literati expression. Blue-green or pale-reddish coloring does not exclude historical literati painting.

### 3. Combined Judgment and Neighboring Styles
1. For historical works, verify biography, context, inscriptions, and collection research before analyzing brushwork and poetry/image relations.
2. For contemporary works, check whether stroke density and wet/dry handling build imagery/rhythm, whether imagery and text/cultural motifs interpret one another, and whether this governs the main work. Together these support **Primary style: contemporary literati-ink expression**. An ink patch or brush heading alone supports **Local borrowing**. Actual mood, cultivation, or copying history remains unknown without external evidence.
3. Compare commercial ink styling and [Neo-Chinese](11-neo-chinese.md): explain what brushwork, imagery, and words jointly express beyond traditional symbols. An unchanged ink splash behind every drink flavor does not support literati dominance.
4. Modern abstract ink/gestural abstraction may overlap. Further attribution needs a specific literati motif, poetry/calligraphy/painting relationship, or historical scheme. If both readings remain viable, describe a boundary or **Mixed** case. Lifelikeness, elegance, and calligraphic quality are not admission gates.

### 4. Worked Attribution Examples

| Case | Input facts → conclusion | Evidence and unresolved questions |
|---|---|---|
| Positive: Wen Zhengming's Garden of the Inept Administrator | The Met records a 1551 ink album of eight garden views and the maker's relationship with the owner. In the fishing-rock leaf, a poem turns fishing into forgetting sorrow and not seeking fish, interpreting rocks/water as retreat rather than a site inventory → historical identity and literati expression supported. | Date/social records and poetry/image interpretation jointly support the claim. High-resolution originals are still needed for detailed texture-stroke analysis; a museum label does not replace formal observation. |
| Negative, invented | Juice packaging has a brush product name, red square seal, and generic splash. Fruit photographs change between flavors but the splash stays unchanged; text gives only ingredients/volume → **Attribution not supported** overall; commercial collage of ink/calligraphic appearances. | The backdrop has no mutual imagery/semantic relation with fruit or copy. This does not judge commercial recognition or the designer's cultivation. |
| Boundary, invented | Anonymous digital album leaves show a low rock in pale dry texture strokes; wind bends bamboo, while dark nodes continue upward. A self-written line reads “暂屈风中身，未改石畔节” (“Though the body bends in wind, its steadfast joints beside the rock remain unchanged”). Maker, date, and tools are absent → literati-like visual/poetic expression supported; history and real-brush manufacture remain unknown. | Wet/dry and density contrasts make temporary yielding with steadfastness visible. Missing identity cannot negate the relationship. Check maker/tools separately. Cropping this into an unrelated promotion and breaking the poetry/image connection would reduce it to Local borrowing. |

## Position and Background

Literati painting spans Song, Yuan, Ming, Qing, and modern periods, with varied techniques and schemes. Ink is not synonymous with literati painting: court and professional makers also used it; nor is the tradition merely monochrome landscape. [The Met's landscape study](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/landscape-painting-in-chinese-art) describes calligraphic lines, imagined retreat, and responses to earlier masters as expressions of cultural identity and inner life.

## Design Philosophy

1. **Express intentions through scenery**: Rocks, bamboo, water, and pavilions carry feeling, ideals, and memory beyond geographic description.
2. **Make writing rhythm perceptible**: Pressure, speed, wetness, tone, and density form the skeleton. Breathing and pauses in strokes do not mean uncontrolled smearing.
3. **Integrate poetry, calligraphy, painting, and seals**: Inscriptions add temporal or emotional turns absent from imagery alone; writing and painted strokes respond spatially. Wen's [1551 album](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39654) exemplifies this shared spiritual space.

## Design Language

> Contemporary applications are suggestions, not commercial standards inherent in historical literati art.

| Dimension | Typical language | Contemporary translation |
|---|---|---|
| Composition and layout | Density/void contrasts and interaction of depicted matter with blank space | Let voids become water, cloud, guidance, or breathing pauses, rather than equal white margins. |
| Color | Ink's wet/dry and tonal ranges, with pale reddish or blue-green colors possible | Use real ink gradations for hierarchy while retaining clear body-text contrast. |
| Typography | Inscriptions/seals respond through location, scale, and stroke direction | Separate expressive calligraphy from readable printed explanation; long text need not use calligraphy. |
| Graphics and symbols | Landscapes, bamboo/rocks, bare trees, pavilions with particular cultural meanings | Establish thematic relevance before selecting motifs; do not pile cranes, clouds, and rocks together. |
| Materials and texture | Paper/silk fibers, ink diffusion, album/handscroll mounting | Show actual fibers/tones in print; limit screen noise so filters do not blur information. |
| Space and interaction | Sequential album leaves and moving handscroll viewing incorporate time | Translate into staged narrative reading while keeping navigation, back controls, and actions discoverable. |

## Practical Design Methods

1. Write one emotional/cultural intention first, such as retaining independent judgment in urban noise; choose imagery and strokes accordingly, before searching stock ink assets.
2. Sketch title location, body column, and image movement together. Calligraphy should balance the composition as an extension, not be pasted arbitrarily on top.
3. Develop one or two core motifs per identity/exhibition, instead of filling every corner with dry brushes, seals, and mountain silhouettes.
4. Let atmosphere serve experience. Corridors, lattice views, and reveal/conceal sequences may evoke album rhythm; digital buttons, forms, and prices remain explicit.

## Design Example

> **Contemporary translation, invented: cultural Citywalk routes and guide system**
> - **Goal**: Slow urban participants down to notice daily nature and historical traces.
> - **Graphics and web**: Each route selects one observation—bamboo shadows by an old wall, moss after rain, or moat reflections—and commissions theme-specific wet/dry ink imagery. A short poem carries meaning; distance, meeting time, and registration use readable modern text. Sequential station stories evoke album leaves, with a persistent overview map and signup button.
> - **Wayfinding**: Timber viewfinders of varied proportions support looking down at moss, across lanes, and up at roofs, translating near/far and solid/void relations into bodily experience.

## Representative Cases and Application Boundaries

- **Case**: Wen Zhengming's [Garden of the Inept Administrator, 1551](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/39654) combines eight ink leaves, facing running-script poems, and a postscript. Museum research reads painting/poetry/calligraphy as expressing understanding of the owner and retreat, beyond garden scenery. Study joint meaning, not a mechanically copied ink-border template.
- **Boundaries**: Strong for literary publishing, humanities exhibitions, tea/incense, arts education, and personally expressive brands. Technical manuals, transport signs, medical warnings, and financial dashboards require precise text/data boundaries even when borrowing ink tones; expressive diffusion cannot mean vague functional information.

## Related Styles and Common Misreadings

- **With [Song-dynasty Objects](07-song-dynasty-objects.md)**: Shared cultural contexts differ in medium: brush/poem expression versus vessel proportion, body/glaze, and use.
- **With [Neo-Chinese](11-neo-chinese.md)**: A specific art-historical tradition differs from a broad contemporary label that may borrow ink, Ming furniture, architecture, or folk craft.
- **Misreading**: Splash background, computer brush font, and red seal do not suffice. Without internal relations among strokes, imagery, and words, even complete traditional symbols remain generic commercial styling.
