# Gothic: Design Philosophy and Style Guide

- **Name**: Gothic
- **Concept type**: Medieval European architecture/art and later formal translations
- **Core definition**: Coordinate pointed arches, ribbed supports, vertical divisions, and colored translucent surfaces into space/viewing, beyond black backgrounds and sharp symbols.

## Attribution Criteria

### 1. Scope and Claims
- **Medieval originals**: Twelfth–sixteenth-century buildings, glass, sculpture, or manuscripts require dates, construction-phase records, and museum research.
- **Revival and contemporary forms**: Eighteenth/nineteenth-century Gothic Revival and current display/object/graphic borrowing may be assessed through ribs, vertical divisions, and tracery, but remain distinct from medieval objects.
- **Dark subculture**: Black leather, skulls, thorns, and crosses belong to another modern symbol system. For book covers/ivories, assess architectural framing of content without demanding real cathedral loads.

### 2. Core Evidence Dimensions

| Core evidence | How to observe or verify | Why it distinguishes | Limits and alternative explanations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pointed arches, ribs, and supports form a continuous architectural system | Trace clustered columns to vault ribs; compare sections showing external supports and openings. | Restores arches to spatial/construction logic rather than pointed decoration on a box. | An interior photo cannot prove forces. Regions use different buttressing; consult building-specific research. |
| Vertical divisions and opening subdivisions continue upward | Mark column lines, branching tracery, and crown/vault terminations in full views. | Identifies progressive branching/closure rather than height alone; skyscraper mullions do not automatically qualify. | Low objects/cases may use niches/tracery narratively without monumental size. |
| Translucent surfaces participate in architectural space | Compare openings/tracery with internal light and circulation. | Skeleton-released walls allow large colored-light fields, beyond an applied filter. | Glass is not mandatory for every Gothic object; windowless woodwork/manuscripts cannot be tested by it. |
| Nonbuilding objects use architectural frameworks for content | Trace niches, colonnettes, and tracery enclosing figures/scenes as repeated narrative units. | Shows architectural organization translated beyond one pointed-arch logo. | Realistic figures or Blackletter alone are insufficient; painting/textiles need medium-specific history. |

- **Supporting appearances**: Black, skulls, thorns, iron crosses, spikes, and Blackletter do not prove historical Gothic form, even together; they often indicate dark subculture. Bright glass and warm ivory can be fully Gothic.

### 3. Combined Judgment and Neighboring Styles
1. In buildings, check whether rib/support, vertical division, and openings/light interlock. A pointed false window in a flat-ceiling room supports **Local borrowing** only.
2. In objects/graphics, check whether niches, columns, and tracery govern divisions/narrative. Classical horizontal orders or diagonal collage with isolated spikes/Blackletter do not support Gothic overall.
3. Compare [Renaissance](15-renaissance.md) and [Classical](13-classical.md): branching ribs/tracery versus orders, round arches, and horizontal bays. Transitional buildings can be **Mixed** by component.
4. A documented nineteenth-century ribbed/glazed church is Gothic Revival: modern date refutes medieval identity, not Gothic form. Without records/sections, date and actual load paths remain unverified.

### 4. Worked Attribution Examples

| Case | Input facts → conclusion | Evidence and unresolved questions |
|---|---|---|
| Positive, invented | A contemporary museum case uses colonnettes carrying pointed niches, exhibits aligned with labels, and upward tracery within the same units; production is documented as modern → **Primary style: contemporary Gothic borrowing**. | Architectural framing organizes exhibits/reading. Furniture needs no cathedral buttresses; modern identity is established. |
| Negative, invented | A metal-festival poster piles skulls, thorns, and a sharp triangle on black with diagonal collage, no niches/tracery/continuous vertical frame → **Attribution not supported** within this historical tradition; dark-subculture visual language is a better direction. | Black and points cannot replace structural divisions. Assess subcultural identity through its own music/visual references. |
| Boundary, invented | A full church interior shows clustered columns, ribs, pointed high windows, and colored light; archives establish nineteenth-century construction → Gothic spatial rules supported, historically Gothic Revival rather than medieval. | Form and records answer different claims. Similar undocumented photos leave medieval versus revival identity unresolved. |

## Position and Background

This guide focuses on approximately twelfth–sixteenth-century European architecture/decorative art, separated from revival and subculture. Pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses formed new support/spatial systems, releasing walls for high stained glass. Sculpture, metal, and ivory developed related language. See [The Met: Gothic Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/gothic-art).

## Design Philosophy

1. **Make skeleton expressive**: Columns, ribs, and supports become a continuous line system tied to actual construction.
2. **Use height and colored light for transcendence**: Vertical divisions guide looking upward; daylight becomes narrative/religious experience through glass.
3. **Match ornament to position/story**: Portal figures, tracery, and miniature scenes correspond to distance, place, and narrative chapter rather than uniformly covering surfaces. Retain that position/meaning awareness in translation.

## Design Language

> Applications are contemporary translations, not historical interface standards.

| Dimension | Typical language | Contemporary translation |
|---|---|---|
| Composition and layout | Vertical extension, pointed frames, progressive branching | Use tall narrow divisions and shared vertical paths for legible reading. |
| Color | Red/blue/gold transmitted light and stone/metal/ivory colors | Use a few jewel tones on clear grounds instead of universal blackness. |
| Typography | Rhythmic Blackletter in manuscripts/inscriptions | Reserve it for suitable Latin headings; use readable modern Chinese headings/body. |
| Graphics and symbols | Tracery, rose windows, natural leaves, narrative compartments | Derive one framework system with main-frame/branch/detail rhythm. |
| Materials and texture | Masonry, translucent glass, wood, ivory, forged metal | Distinguish light surfaces, supports, and fine carving rather than covering everything in fake stone. |
| Space and interaction | Height sequences, thresholds, changing light with movement | Stage digital chapters without forcing long animations before essential content. |

## Practical Design Methods

1. Define historical dignity, craft, or fantasy before selecting imagery; do not indiscriminately apply religious signs or skulls to ordinary brands.
2. Build a vertical reading skeleton for image/title/information first. Restrict complex tracery to crowns or chapter nodes.
3. Concentrate atmospheric frames/light in heroes, exhibit frames, and transitions; keep body text, search, and ticket forms clear.
4. Test real light at entry/stopping/upward viewpoints. Prototype translucent materials under daylight or gallery lights rather than trusting renders alone.

## Design Example

> **Contemporary translation, invented: medieval stained-glass exhibition**
> - **Goal**: Explain narrative through light and framed divisions.
> - **Graphics/web**: Three tall poster bands translate a triple window: glass details center, title left, dates/guide right. Online, static high-resolution images and tracery zoom are immediately available without long transitions.
> - **Space**: A dark threshold leads to a tall luminous hall; backlit cases recreate colored light. Light/structure explain the works beyond generic mystery from black type/background.

## Representative Cases and Application Boundaries

- **Cases**: Saint-Denis is foundational for integrated pointed arches, ribs, and glass. [V&A's introduction](https://www.vam.ac.uk/articles/the-gothic-style-an-introduction) spans pointed arches, rose windows, wood, and ivory diptychs.
- **Boundaries**: Useful for historical exhibitions, craft narratives, serious theater, and specifically grounded fantasy. Dense dashboards, long academic text, and small forms should borrow hierarchical branching without copying intricate borders/Blackletter.

## Related Styles and Common Misreadings

- **With [Classical](13-classical.md)**: Order proportions/horizontal entablatures differ from pointed ribs and upward translucent spaces.
- **With [Renaissance](15-renaissance.md)**: Classical orders, round arches, and cross-direction modules progressively replaced medieval branching systems.
- **Misreading**: Spikes, skulls, bats, and black cannot collapse medieval craft, nineteenth-century revival, and late-twentieth-century subculture into one category.
