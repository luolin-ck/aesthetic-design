# Mediterranean: Design Philosophy and Style Guide

- **English name**: Mediterranean style
- **Concept type**: Multi-regional architectural references and contemporary interior label
- **Core definition**: Relationships among people, sunlight, particular places, local materials, and indoor/outdoor living transitions, rather than a fixed blue-and-white-arch palette.

---

## Attribution Criteria

### 1. Scope and Claims
Mediterranean coasts span southern Europe, North Africa, and western Asia, with different climates, stones, and building traditions. They are neither one ethnicity nor a uniform historical school. Establish three premises:
- **Name the regional branch and comparator**: A generic seaside-holiday impression cannot judge everything. Sifnos in the Greek Cyclades uses adjoining white cubes, flat roofs, and stone courtyards; see [Municipality of Sifnos: Architecture](https://sifnos.gr/en/architecture/). Mallorca includes sequences of local yellow sandstone masses such as Utzon's Can Lis. Mallorcan stone houses need not be blue and white.
- **Separate visual/spatial translation from geographical origin**: Judge borrowing from a specific regional spatial/material language, **not whether a project physically stands on the Mediterranean or uses locally quarried stone**.
- **Respect medium limits**: Graphics and websites may cite regional colors, tiles, and light imagery; screenshots cannot establish a physical building's climate performance.

### 2. Core Evidence Dimensions

| Core evidence dimension | How to observe or verify | Why it discriminates | Limits and alternative explanations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Massing and openings correspond to the selected regional reference** | Compare named local buildings, identifying roof forms, apparent wall depth, openings, and massing relationships. | Replaces generic seaside atmosphere and universal white-wall/arch stereotypes with verifiable regional vocabulary. | Photographs cannot establish dates, location, or true structural wall thickness. Different branches need not all appear together. |
| **Indoor/outdoor transitions organize main activities** | Use plans and full views to trace rooms, sheltered galleries, courtyards, or terraces; see how seats relate to openings and shade edges. | Identifies a living-space type responding to sun and sea breezes, beyond a sea photograph on an ordinary wall. | Courtyards are not exclusive to the region; combine with regional forms/materials. Interior-only projects can translate window or transition zones locally. |
| **Material appearances and craft patterns extend the particular branch** | Compare plaster, stone, terracotta, or painted tiles in walls, floors, skirtings, and pools with regional patterns and divisions. | Distinguishes sourced regional traditions from arbitrary mixtures of blue paint, pots, and exotic symbols. | Modern imitation stone or manufactured tiles do not cancel visible borrowing. Actual source, plaster recipes, and handwork require records; natural material is not a qualification. |
| **Principal surfaces continue one branch or explicitly layered combinations** | Connect walls, openings, floors, and fixed furniture to show consistent regional relationships. | Distinguishes a dominant spatial language from one souvenir tile in a kitchen. | Cross-regional/style combinations are possible, such as Mallorcan architecture with Boho furnishings. Identify each layer separately; harmonious comfort is not region-specific. |

- **Common supporting appearances**: Blue/white stripes, semicircular arches, anchors, ship wheels, shells, plastic olive trees. Individually or together they offer **insufficient evidence of Mediterranean architecture or space**. Warm sandstone houses in Mallorca or southern France with straight lintels need not contain blue-white arches.

### 3. Combined Judgment and Neighboring Styles
1. **Verify named region → massing/openings → indoor/outdoor transitions → material interfaces**:
   - Identify a specific reference, such as the Cyclades, Mallorca, or Amalfi. Check whether form, transitional settings, and wall/floor language organize the main space. Interior-only projects need not reproduce an entire building but must identify the translated layers.
   - Standard office glazing and desks with a blue-white logo and shells support only **Local borrowing of coastal imagery**. Narrow detail images require **Insufficient evidence**.
2. **Challenge neighboring explanations**:
   - Compare **[Bohemian](24-bohemian.md)**: Do regional building references generate boundaries and indoor/outdoor sequences, or do cross-cultural unmatched textiles and collections generate the setting? A Mediterranean shell and Boho furnishings can coexist as **Mixed** layers.
   - Compare **modern Coastal Style**: Airy light, white walls, and sandy fabrics without regional forms or material/craft references fit a broader coastal category; do not automatically narrow to Mediterranean.

### 4. Worked Attribution Examples

| Example type | Input facts → conclusion | Evidence and outstanding questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive example (hypothetical)** | A new seaside lodging names Sifnos settlements as references. Adjoining white flat-roofed cubes open to stone courtyards and sheltered masonry seats; wall/floor joints and path divisions correspond to local cases → **Primary style: Mediterranean borrowing from the Cycladic branch governs building and courtyard**. | **Evidence**: Massing, roofs, transitions, and paving align with named regional records without added blue windows or arches. **Outstanding**: This confirms design correspondence, not physical location in Greece. |
| **Counterexample (hypothetical)** | An inland office retains standard aluminum/glass partitions and identical panel desks, changing only its logo to blue/white and adding shells and an Aegean painting → **Local borrowing of coastal symbols; Mediterranean spatial attribution is not supported**. | **Evidence**: Massing, opening transitions, and wall/floor interfaces lack regional organization. **Outstanding**: Shell origin need not be checked to identify decoration. |
| **Boundary example (hypothetical)** | A residence's shell and sheltered galleries correspond to a named Mallorcan sandstone/framed-view case, while its living room is governed by unmatched colorful fringed rugs, woven chairs, and old wood cabinets → **Mixed: Mallorcan Mediterranean architecture/courtyard with Boho furnishings**. | **Evidence**: Distinct architectural and furnishing layers explain the combination; absent blue/white does not weaken the Mallorcan reference. **Outstanding**: Quarry origin and actual climate zone require project records rather than photo inference. |

## Position and Background

Mediterranean countries and regions differ in history, climate, and craft; they do not form one ethnic or doctrinal school. Architectural Digest's contemporary interior overview covers Greek, Spanish, Italian, Moroccan, and other coastal traditions, from white plaster to terracotta and painted tiles. See [Architectural Digest: Mediterranean Interior Design: Everything You Need to Know](https://www.architecturaldigest.com/story/mediterranean-interior-design-101). This guide begins with how a particular place responds to climate and shapes daily dwelling.

---

## Design Philosophy

> These are contemporary spatial and brand-design interpretations.

1. **Start with sunlight and patterns of staying**: Identify where people sit, pass, enter, and look out during the day; derive opening sizes, shade depths, and fixed-seat orientations.
2. **Let local making create identity**: Stone, plaster, and clay acquire character through light and construction, not wholesale plastic shells, anchors, and striped cloth.
3. **Provide settings for gathering and solitude**: Outdoor dining, shaded afternoon conversation, and quiet reading need suitable scales, enclosure, and lighting rather than one unshaded hall for every event.

---

## Design Language

> These are not compulsory rules shared by all Mediterranean regions. Select a particular reference and respond to actual project conditions.

| Design dimension | Typical characteristics | Contemporary application |
|---|---|---|
| **Composition and layout** | Courtyards, deep openings, framed galleries, layered indoor/outdoor sightlines. | Use spatial photographs with depth to guide reading, rather than place all text directly over a seascape. |
| **Color** | Lime mortar, stone, terracotta, coastal vegetation, and local painted crafts. | Sample the chosen site/materials; blue/white, terracotta, or strong glazed colors each need a source. |
| **Typography** | No single historical Mediterranean typeface. | Use reliable Latin/Chinese families and readable body text rather than exaggerated holiday handwriting. |
| **Graphics and symbols** | Painted geometric/botanical tiles, including azulejos, iron lattices, local building contours. | Verify regional origins and original architectural uses before translating auxiliary packaging graphics. |
| **Materials and texture** | Stone, hand-applied plaster, timber beams, and terracotta related to local climate. | Choose for body contact and weather exposure; do not cover every surface in one cheap antique-tile image. |
| **Space and interaction** | Transitional sequences and masonry resting places for meals and cooling. | Organize bookings/guides by morning, afternoon, evening, or actual experiences; keep prices and availability direct. |

---

## Practical Design Methods

1. **Fix a region and living situation in the brief**: Name the island or coastal area and actual habit; replace vague romantic-seaside requests with concrete references.
2. **Build narratives from real local features**: Menus, packaging, and imagery should connect ingredients, tableware, and buildings rather than mix Greek, Spanish, and Moroccan symbols without logic.
3. **Resolve sun, ventilation, and circulation first**: Address western sun, cross-ventilation, and furniture movement before optional arches or tiles. Do not reshape every opening solely for photographs.
4. **Make photography support booking**: Separate room areas, food services, and transport information clearly. Use spatial light photographs to explain experience rather than rough plaster background textures that obstruct orders.

## Design Example

> **Contemporary translation (hypothetical): a small coastal stone lodging for slow travelers**
> - **Goal**: Communicate honest local architecture and daily experience rather than generic social-media scenery.
> - **Space and materials**: Restore traditional rubble walls and a flat-roofed courtyard. Provide morning-lit rooms, shaded afternoon reading, and evening communal courtyard dining at different scales. Select local stone and plain clay tiles for slip resistance and marine weathering, without requiring blue-white paint.
> - **Brand and website**: Draw colors from warm sandstone, fig leaves, and handmade clay dishes. Lead with island transport, room plans, and bookable dates, then document daily light changes and local crafts with actual photographs.

## Representative Cases and Application Boundaries

- **Representative case**: Jørn Utzon's Can Lis in Mallorca, completed in 1971, uses local yellow Marés sandstone, four sea-facing volumes, deep shaded openings, and semi-outdoor courtyards to reinterpret local construction and family life. It demonstrates why Mediterranean architecture is broader than blue/white arches. See [Utzon Foundation: History — Can Lis](https://utzon.foundation/history).
- **Application boundaries**: Suitable for projects genuinely connected to local culture, coastal lodging, food, and traditional building crafts. Inland, cold-climate, or dense urban brands can borrow light and transitional-space methods without inventing seaside identity. Physical designs must respect actual insulation, moisture, and climate requirements rather than simply copy photographs.

---

## Related Styles and Common Misreadings

- **Related styles**:
  - **Mediterranean vs [Bohemian](24-bohemian.md)**: Boho begins with unmatched collections and layered textiles; Mediterranean design begins with regional massing, framed openings, transitions, and building materials.
  - **Mediterranean vs [Nordic](20-nordic.md)**: Both attend to life and materials, but respond to different light/climate, spatial sequences, and making traditions.
- **Common misreading**: Using blue/white paint, arches, anchors, and shells as a universal definition, allowing one archipelago's postcard image to erase regional diversity.
