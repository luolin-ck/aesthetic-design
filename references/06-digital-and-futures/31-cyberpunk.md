# Cyberpunk: Design Philosophy and Style Guide

- **English name**: Cyberpunk
- **Concept type**: Science-fiction subgenre and its literary, cinematic, and visual culture
- **Core definition**: Conflicts among advanced technologies, power structures, and daily life question individual survival and autonomy in technologized societies.

---

## Attribution Criteria

### 1. Scope and Claims
Separate three claims:
- **Narrative genre**: For novels, films, or games, inspect how plots connect technological systems, power, and characters' circumstances. One screenshot cannot establish an entire work's literary/cinematic genre.
- **Cyberpunk visual language**: For posters, concept scenes, clothing, or interfaces, examine visible relationships among systems, environments, and individual scale. A complete novel's plot and the creator's reference list are unnecessary for visual comparison.
- **Actual sources or political positions**: Named influence or particular criticism requires interviews, production notes, or research. Light colors alone do not establish it.

### 2. Core Evidence Dimensions

| Core evidence dimension | How to observe or verify | Why it discriminates | Limits and alternative explanations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Technology visibly acts on bodies, actions, or personal space** | Identify whom screens, networks, prostheses, or infrastructure affect, and how they change/restrict action, rather than list symbols. | Places technology within human circumstances, unlike generic glowing tech imagery. | One poster can suggest tension through scale, occlusion, or action; full social institutions need not be explained. Deeper narrative context requires text. |
| **System scale conflicts with individual scale, resources, or permissions** | Compare giant advertisements/infrastructure with small figures, access messages with users, advanced interfaces with improvised repairs. | Makes the high-technology/individual-condition tension observable beyond smooth homogeneous futurism. | No single conflict image is mandatory; its absence cannot exclude a narrative supported by sufficient plot evidence. |
| **Technology enters environments marked by living and use** | Examine signs, ads, repaired services, wear, crowding, or old infrastructure beside advanced devices. | Makes technological/daily friction visible rather than an isolated product render or neon background. | Bright, dry, clean settings can qualify. Other scene languages can construct friction without copying a particular film. |
| **These relationships govern image or narrative** | Compare subjects, settings, and sequences, or read how characters depend on systems, are controlled, or excluded. | Separates a main visual/narrative framework from limited technology decoration. | Concept art cannot reveal the complete story. Usability and render polish are quality questions, not affiliation requirements. |

- **Common supporting appearances**: Neon, blue-purple light, rainy nights, code noise, glitches. Alone or combined, they give **insufficient evidence of a primary Cyberpunk language or genre**. A smooth product with colored light strips does not qualify on that basis. Monochrome, neon-free, dry daylight scenes can qualify when system/body/access tensions are clear.

### 3. Combined Judgment and Neighboring Styles
1. **Judgment sequence**:
   - **Visual route**: Inspect intervention in bodies or living circumstances, then continuing environment, scale, and access relationships. If these govern the main image/series, conclude **Primary style: Cyberpunk visual language**. Neon and code borders on an ordinary image are **Local borrowing of technological/cyber vocabulary**.
   - **Narrative route**: Inspect story and context separately. A poster without plot means **Insufficient evidence of narrative genre**, without negating supported visual borrowing.
2. **Challenge neighboring explanations**:
   - Compare **[Y2K](33-y2k.md)**: Is the emphasis on systems/human circumstances, or millennium digital futures through virtual objects, metal sheen, and streamlined devices?
   - Compare **[Retrofuturism](32-retrofuturism.md)**: Is there a second time layer looking back from the present at a past future? Categories may overlap; do not force exclusivity.

### 4. Worked Attribution Examples

| Example type | Input facts → conclusion | Evidence and outstanding questions |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive example (hypothetical)** | A science-fiction festival poster places a huge aerial transit screen above the city; its access message blocks a waiting passenger's route, and advanced terminals connect to patched old street infrastructure → **Primary style: Cyberpunk visually; the film's narrative genre still has Insufficient evidence**. | **Evidence**: Infrastructure, access denial, and individual scale organize the main picture. **Outstanding**: Read the script/film before classifying the entire narrative. |
| **Counterexample (hypothetical)** | An earphone ad shows a smooth product amid blue-purple light strips, rain, and decorative code, without people, living context, or system effects → **Attribution not supported for Cyberpunk style or genre; technological/cyber vocabulary only**. | **Evidence**: Isolated product and lights do not establish system/human relationships. **Outstanding**: Reassess if later campaign scenes introduce a developed world. |
| **Boundary example (hypothetical)** | A monochrome game scene has no neon: a character connects a prosthetic limb to a paid street terminal; insufficient access blocks movement, and later text explains dependence on corporate body-modification services → **Cyberpunk visual and narrative directions are supported; missing neon is not counterevidence**. | **Evidence**: Prosthesis, commercial permissions, and restricted movement form the central tension. **Outstanding**: Historical lineage and actual references require production records, not color assumptions. |

## Position and Background

Cyberpunk developed as a science-fiction literary/cinematic worldview before broad adoption in graphics, spaces, and digital design. The Academy Museum of Motion Pictures' [Cyberpunk: Envisioning Possible Futures Through Cinema](https://www.academymuseum.org/en/exhibitions/cyberpunk) examines power in advanced societies, marginalized viewpoints, and diverse futures. Neon cities are a common entrance, while identity, dependence, and social relations also support the language.

---

## Design Philosophy

1. **Put technology into particular human circumstances**: Define who controls systems, depends on them, or is excluded, then determine screens, prostheses, and network interfaces.
2. **Make new and old life traces rub together**: Advanced devices can coexist with worn parts, crowded lanes, and repairs, giving a world believable use histories.
3. **Create scale tension from an individual viewpoint**: Infrastructure and data systems need perceptible personal scale so control/autonomy becomes legible.

---

## Design Language

> These common features and contemporary translations are not compulsory for every Cyberpunk work.

| Design dimension | Typical characteristics | Contemporary application |
|---|---|---|
| **Composition and layout** | Urban depth, unequal scales, giant ads beside small figures. | Establish a system/individual contrast while keeping narrative and reading focus clear. |
| **Color** | Artificial sources, deep shadows, strong warm/cool interactions. | Set light-source logic before accents; avoid equally glaring brightness everywhere. |
| **Typography** | Street signs, system displays, advertisements, multilingual layers. | Design by fictional institution and sign function; protect a stable readable information layer. |
| **Graphics and symbols** | Networks, surveillance fields, prostheses, infrastructure, permissions. | Explain world rules rather than add meaningless code or fake text noise. |
| **Materials and texture** | Advanced smooth surfaces beside wear, oxidation, and repairs. | Age materials by actual fictional use rather than apply one dirt filter everywhere. |
| **Space and interaction** | Layered cities intertwine screens and bodily movement. | Keep exhibition routes/signs clear; separate fictional atmosphere from real controls digitally. |

---

## Practical Design Methods

1. **Write a concise world/institution premise first**: Explain how technology changes travel, work, or identity checks before drawing.
2. **Organize around one conflict**: For example, person versus surveillance or giant ads versus street survival; make other elements support it.
3. **Give multilingual signs meaning and purpose**: Use genuine language/function logic rather than treat a foreign language as exotic texture.
4. **Protect interface and wayfinding usability**: Strong frames/local glow may work, but buttons, body text, errors, and states must stay legible; ambient light must not obscure safety/direction signs.

## Design Example

> **Contemporary translation (hypothetical): a nighttime urban science-fiction festival**
> - **Goal**: Express individual choice in a data-driven city while supporting efficient ticket purchase.
> - **Print and tickets**: A giant transit screen dwarfs a street-level passenger. Bright glow concentrates on fictional notices; readable festival schedules occupy darker nearby areas. Physical tickets resemble urban travel passes.
> - **Website**: A layered city creates atmosphere on the opening screen; filters, schedules, and checkout use standardized high-contrast components for direct purchase.

## Representative Cases and Application Boundaries

- **Representative case**: *Blade Runner* (1982) intertwines massive buildings, ads, artificial light, and individual survival. [BFI's analysis](https://www.bfi.org.uk/features/blade-runner) discusses Film Noir influences and Syd Mead's visual work. It is an important reference, not a universal template for all periods/regions.
- **Application boundaries**: Useful for science-fiction film/games, exhibitions, and cultural projects examining technology and society. In daily productivity, health, or public services, strong glare, glitches, and alarm-like effects can confuse real states and increase cognitive burden.

---

## Related Styles and Common Misreadings

- **Related styles**:
  - **Cyberpunk vs [Retrofuturism](32-retrofuturism.md)**: Systems/human tension differs from retrospective time relationships, though they can overlap.
  - **Cyberpunk vs [Y2K](33-y2k.md)**: Y2K draws on millennium digital culture, translucent hardware, and streamlined future imagery without requiring harsh social-power conflict.
- **Common misreading**: Adding blue-purple glow, rain, code, and a science-fiction name—or making those props mandatory. Define technology, institutions, and people before visual form.
