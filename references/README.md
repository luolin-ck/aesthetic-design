# Aesthetic Design Reference Handbook

These 36 independent guides begin with attribution criteria and then explain design practice.

1. **Attribution criteria:** core evidence, combined judgments, alternative explanations, and positive, negative, and boundary examples.
2. **Philosophy and practical design:** historical context, design ideas, visual language, and contemporary reinterpretation across graphics, products, spaces, and interfaces.

The six groups are retrieval aids, not mutually exclusive art-historical classes. The entries mix schools, movements, periods, regional traditions, aesthetic concepts, and market labels. Bauhaus denotes a historical school and its influence; "Chinese style" is much broader, so this collection offers specific entries for Song objects, Literati Ink, Ming furniture, court decoration, and Neo-Chinese design. Neither the 36 entries nor their selected branches exhaust design history.

For the operational workflow, use [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). This handbook expands its reasoning and examples. Start visual comparison at the [materials entry point](../materials/README.md) and [catalog](../materials/catalog.json). Each guide links its same-numbered `materials/styles/` note, which names real works and identifies sources, visible evidence, branches, and limits. Follow catalog asset IDs to the actual local images and records. Both language versions use the same materials. Local images can be inspected offline; an external-only candidate or missing image leaves a stated visual-evidence gap. External citations provide provenance and optional enrichment, not proof that a page or image was inspected.

## 1. Attribution method: define the object before naming the style

### 1.1 Define the object and the question

Specify whether the evidence concerns one chair, an entire exhibition hall, a poster layout, or a whole website. Do not infer the whole interior from one corner or the whole website's layout from a product photograph.

Distinguish three questions:

- **Historical identity:** is this an original design/work associated with a particular school, movement, period, or region? Check the relevant records.
- **Contemporary formal language:** which observable organizing relationships does the finished object use? Compare visible structure.
- **Actual process or influence:** did the creator use a particular reference or workshop method? This causal claim needs process or documentary evidence.

For an unlabeled input, record visible relationships before proposing candidates. For a labeled input, test the label against plausible alternatives rather than starting from its conclusion. Expand reading when necessary; if the direction lies outside this collection, acknowledge that coverage limit rather than forcing a listed style.

### 1.2 Separate visible features from facts requiring verification

Lines, color fields, component connections, image/text hierarchy, repeat patterns, and spatial sequences may be directly observable.

Dates, authorship, institutional links, actual techniques, material composition, natural aging, and creative references require collection records, research, material evidence, or process documentation. A screenshot cannot establish those facts by itself.

Evidence must concern the object being judged. A designer's school attendance, or a museum's ownership of one early work, does not assign every later work to the same historical institution.

### 1.3 Answer historical identity and formal language separately

For historical identity:

- **Attribution established:** identify the records supporting the design/work's identity, such as dates and workshop documentation.
- **Insufficient historical evidence:** specify which authorship, date, commission, or scholarly record is missing.
- **Direct counterevidence:** state exactly which identity claim the record contradicts.
- **Original design versus the present object:** if reliable records show manufacture in 2026, do not call that object an original made in 1925. It may still be a later production or reproduction of a historical design. Original design, later mass production, modern reproduction, authorization, and authenticity are different claims.

For formal language, compare organizing relationships with the guide's core evidence and relevant historical examples. A viewer need not obtain the creator's reference list before making a formal comparison. Claiming that the creator actually used that reference still requires separate evidence.

Historical attribution can be established while current-object provenance remains unknown; formal language can be clear while actual creative influence is undocumented.

### 1.4 Find organizing relationships and compare neighbors

Use each guide's evidence table to identify which structural, proportional, component, pattern, or image/text relationships jointly govern the main object. Do not simply count colors, materials, or symbols.

Ask:

- Can the nearest neighboring style explain the same work?
- What conclusion changes if a key relationship changes?
- Does the relationship still make sense after reinterpretation in another medium?
- Are the candidate and its neighbor overlapping categories or broader/narrower descriptions?

Ornament can organize the image; removal of ornament is not a universal attribution test. Attractive, expensive, usable, or materially authentic does not mean stylistically qualified. A poorly organized imitation may still visibly use a style, while a fine object may not belong to its advertised category.

### 1.5 State evidence, conclusions, and unknowns

Use the following terms for **formal attribution**, with a specified object and scope. Report historical identity and actual influence separately. Missing information is not counterevidence.

| Formal conclusion | When it applies | Minimum explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary style** | Identifiable rules jointly govern the main object | Which relationships, where they operate, and why a narrower attribution is justified; "primary" describes coverage and governing role, not quality |
| **Local borrowing** | A region, graphic, or component corresponds to the language without governing the whole | What is borrowed and what organizes the whole; if only colors resemble one another, say color resemblance |
| **Mixed** | Two or more rule sets perform meaningful roles | Identify their respective locations and how they divide roles or jointly constrain the same structure; two keywords alone do not establish fusion |
| **Attribution not supported** | Main visible relationships conflict with the formal claim, or direct counterevidence applies | The specific conflict, limited to the inspected scope; historical identity/dates receive their own conclusion |
| **Insufficient evidence** | The input cannot distinguish reasonable formal explanations | The missing full view, component detail, plan, or content; a missing date alone does not undo an adequate formal comparison |

These are working terms, not official certification or a "hit enough features" score. Usability, accessibility, fabrication, and aesthetic quality remain separate questions.

A useful formal conclusion reads: "For [object/scope] at the [visual layer], the conclusion is [term], because [relationships and evidence]. Its relationship to [neighbor] is [distinction, overlap, or unresolved ambiguity]. Missing evidence relevant to this claim is [if any]." If the explanation contains only "clean," "luxurious," "refined," or "Chinese elements," return to the evidence tables.

## 2. Worked comparison: what does a red/yellow/blue grid establish?

The following three cases are **constructed demonstrations**, not documented historical commissions. Start with the input's actual visual/documentary facts rather than reasoning backward from a label.

| Input and key change | Conclusion | Evidence and unresolved claims |
|---|---|---|
| **Case 1: a 2026 course poster.** Red/yellow/blue fields appear. Time columns, course names, and descriptions share alignment axes; type size/weight encode hierarchy; photographs fit the column system. Long titles in the series reflow within the same columns. | **Primarily [Swiss Style](01-rational-modern/03-swiss-style.md) typography.** Primary colors do not by themselves establish [Bauhaus](01-rational-modern/01-bauhaus.md) or [De Stijl](01-rational-modern/02-de-stijl.md). | The governing system is columns, typographic information coding, and image/text alignment. Grids and primary colors are shared clues. Actual references remain unknown; the stated date identifies a contemporary work rather than a historical movement original. |
| **Case 2: change the governing relationship.** Keep the colors, remove equal information columns, and introduce unequal orthogonal lines/planes extending toward the edges. Text becomes a secondary corner note; nonfigurative field size, direction, and empty-space tension create asymmetric balance. | **Primarily [De Stijl-like](01-rational-modern/02-de-stijl.md) formal language.** The earlier Swiss-style information hierarchy no longer governs the object. | The governing relationship changed from information coding to abstract balancing of planes and voids. The distinction does not come from a color change or a new label. This remains a contemporary formal comparison, not historical movement identity. |
| **Case 3: incomplete evidence and mismatched attribution.** Only a crop is visible, obscuring text and layout. Its filename says "Bauhaus 1923." A genuine museum collection link accompanies it, but that record depicts a different object. | **Insufficient historical evidence.** Describe only the visible formal fragment; neither a filename nor another authentic object's record certifies the screenshot. | Historical evidence must match the object. Request the complete view and matching authorship/date/collection records if those claims matter. |

**Documented comparison:** the [Bauhaus guide](01-rational-modern/01-bauhaus.md) discusses Marianne Brandt's teapot, with silver-colored metal and dark ebony rather than red/yellow/blue. Records establish its circa-1924 design and Bauhaus metal-workshop context. Its vessel, spout, and insulating handle also form legible geometric components for containing, pouring, and holding. Primary colors are therefore neither sufficient proof nor a necessary condition for a documented Bauhaus original. A particular teapot offered for sale still needs its own manufacturing and provenance evidence.

## 3. Reading and practical design

1. **Define the practical problem first:** medium, audience, and core task, such as finding exhibition registration within five seconds.
2. **Read criteria → philosophy → language → methods:** establish evidence and misleading surface clues before selecting visual treatments.
3. **Turn ideas into inspectable actions:** specify alignment, component connections, sequence, and image/text roles instead of collecting only palettes and vintage assets.
4. **Reinterpret for the medium:** historical furniture, architecture, and painting did not prescribe digital controls. Interface advice here is contemporary reinterpretation and must preserve readable, usable interactions.
5. **Test real content and contexts:** use actual short/long text, product images, material samples, or operation flows where available. Disclose sample content and untested states.
6. **Verify the finished artifact:** return it to the attribution workflow, point to its actual evidence, compare neighbors, and revise unsupported claims or design decisions. If a required target style remains unmet, say the requirement remains unfinished.

Each guide contains nine sections: **Attribution Criteria; Position and Background; Design Philosophy; Design Language; Practical Design Methods; Design Example; Representative Cases and Application Boundaries; Related Styles and Common Misreadings; Sources**.

Cases labeled "constructed" demonstrate reasoning or reinterpretation rather than historical projects. The criteria, color/type suggestions, and interface rules are practical guidance, not universal historical regulations.

## 5. Quick comparisons

Use these questions to select and compare guides. They are not mutually exclusive certification rules.

| Neighbors | What to check first |
|---|---|
| [Bauhaus](01-rational-modern/01-bauhaus.md) / [De Stijl](01-rational-modern/02-de-stijl.md) | Bauhaus-like contemporary attribution needs specific historical component/layout correspondence; function alone is not enough. De Stijl-like language concerns independent planes/lines and asymmetric balancing, including orthogonal and diagonal branches. Check historical identity separately. |
| [Bauhaus](01-rational-modern/01-bauhaus.md) / [Swiss Style](01-rational-modern/03-swiss-style.md) | Swiss Style focuses on column systems, typographic information coding, and shared image/text axes. Primary colors on a modern grid do not by themselves establish Bauhaus. |
| [Art Nouveau](05-decoration-and-experimentation/25-art-nouveau.md) / [Art Deco](05-decoration-and-experimentation/26-art-deco.md) | Compare the generation of connected contours and ornamental divisions with axial, radiating, stepped, repeated, or streamlined decorative rhythm. Check the regional branch; curved versus straight is not a valid universal split. |
| [Baroque](03-western-classics/16-baroque.md) / [Rococo](03-western-classics/17-rococo.md) | Compare movement/depth/light converging into a dramatic event with local C/S rocaille relationships generating boundaries. Do not divide by public/private use or large/small scale; both can coexist in different layers. |
| [Minimalism](01-rational-modern/05-minimalism.md) / [Wabi-sabi](02-eastern-traditions/12-wabi-sabi.md) | Compare reduced modules and precise spacing with restrained viewing focused on individual variation and time-related traces. Neutral colors alone establish neither; use the relevant medium and branch. |
| [Y2K](06-digital-and-futures/33-y2k.md) / [Vaporwave](06-digital-and-futures/34-vaporwave.md) | Compare virtual streamlined/device-like shaping with loops, distortion, and temporal collage of old media. Do not infer classification or personal beliefs from optimistic/melancholic mood; both rule sets can jointly organize a work. |
| Architectural Brutalism / [Web Brutalism](06-digital-and-futures/36-web-brutalism.md) | Architectural history concerns physical structure and documented context. In web design, distinguish raw document appearance, hard-edged Neobrutalist components, and disrupted reading conventions. Appearance alone proves neither architectural identity nor a designer's intention. |

## 6. Sources and application boundaries

- Each guide names its sources, the claims those sources support, and the original access dates. References principally include museums, archives, academic/cultural institutions, creators' original records, and specialist publications.
- Historical claims must stay within their cited evidence. Contemporary design guidance and constructed briefs are practical reinterpretations, not standards for historical restoration.
- A style label cannot substitute for functional suitability, accessibility, material safety, fabrication quality, or cultural-context judgment.
- In a mixed design, identify the rules contributed by each style and how they work together. Division by layer is one option; jointly governing the same structure is another. Do not require one contribution to become mere surface decoration.
- The translated documents retain the original references and their access dates. Translation does not constitute a fresh check of every external webpage or an empirical image-classification evaluation.
